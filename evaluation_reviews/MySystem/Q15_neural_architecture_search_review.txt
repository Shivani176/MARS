# Neural Architecture Search: A Structured Literature Review

## 1. INTRODUCTION

Neural Architecture Search (NAS) has emerged as a critical area of research aimed at automating the design of optimal neural network architectures [1,4,5]. The field addresses the fundamental challenge of finding effective architectures within predefined search spaces, which are often extremely large and computationally expensive to explore [5,7].

## 2. KEY FINDINGS

### Computational Efficiency Improvements
Multiple studies demonstrate significant computational cost reductions through various optimization strategies. Progressive evolution approaches achieve better results on CIFAR-10 and CIFAR-100 while using significantly less computational resources compared to previous evolutionary algorithm-based methods [1]. Weight-sharing mechanisms combined with sequential model-based optimization lead to increased search efficiency while retaining competitiveness of found architectures [7]. Training-free methods show particular promise, with one approach achieving state-of-the-art performance of 94.21% using less than 1/13 computational cost of baseline methods on NASBench-101 [6].

### Performance Achievements
Several methods report substantial performance improvements over baseline approaches. GPT-based evolutionary search improves the performance of finely tuned neural architectures by up to about 12% compared to those without GPT integration [4]. Multi-objective genetic algorithms for Transformer architectures demonstrate that searched neural network structures outperform all baseline models when considering both BLEU scores and perplexity metrics [9]. Hardware-accelerator co-design approaches achieve 4.4x energy-delay product (EDP) reduction with 2.7% accuracy improvement on ImageNet compared to human-designed systems [11].

### Architecture Representation Learning
Research reveals that unsupervised architecture representation learning considerably improves downstream architecture search efficiency [2]. Pre-training architecture representations using only neural architectures without their accuracies as labels helps map neural architectures with similar performance to the same regions in latent space, making transitions relatively smooth and benefiting diverse downstream search strategies [2]. Graph-based approaches demonstrate that network architecture performance can be predicted through graph properties, enabling remarkably efficient searching processes [3].

## 3. METHODOLOGICAL APPROACHES

### Search Strategies
The literature presents diverse search methodologies including evolutionary algorithms with progressive space reduction [1], genetic algorithms combined with generative pre-trained models [4], and dominative subspace mining approaches [5]. Sequential model-based optimization with weight-sharing represents another prominent strategy [7], while reinforcement learning agents serve as meta-controllers for network transformation [12].

### Evaluation Frameworks
Common evaluation datasets include CIFAR-10, CIFAR-100 [1,7], ImageNet [6,11], and specialized benchmarks like NASBench-101 [6] and DeepNets-1M [10]. Performance metrics vary by application domain, encompassing accuracy measures for image classification, BLEU scores and perplexity for translation tasks [9], and energy-delay product for hardware acceleration [11].

### Architectural Constraints
Several approaches focus on specific architectural domains including plain convolutional networks without skip-connections [12], Spiking Transformers for energy-efficient computation [14], and attention mechanism optimization [9]. Training-free methods utilize Floating-Point Operations (FLOPs) as performance metrics independent of model computations and training dynamics [14].

## 4. RESEARCH GAPS

### Interpretability Limitations
Existing methods heavily rely on black-box controllers, suffering from serious problems of lacking interpretability [6]. While disentangled approaches attempt to address this by separating architecture representations into semantically meaningful concepts [6], broader interpretability challenges remain across the field.

### Search Space Constraints
Current NAS methods still require human input to expand search spaces and cannot generate entirely new architectures [10]. The structural properties of neural architectures are difficult to preserve in latent space when architecture representation learning and search are coupled, resulting in less effective search performance [2].

### Scalability and Generalization
Despite promising results, few neural architectures obtained from NAS methods achieve human-level performances in practical applications, primarily due to huge search spaces making NAS algorithms inefficient [4]. The challenge of maintaining performance while reducing computational requirements remains a persistent issue across multiple studies [1,5,7].

## 5. CONCLUSION

The literature collectively demonstrates that Neural Architecture Search has evolved from computationally prohibitive approaches to more efficient methods through innovations in progressive search, weight-sharing, and representation learning. While significant performance improvements and computational cost reductions have been achieved, challenges remain in interpretability, search space expansion, and practical deployment of discovered architectures.
