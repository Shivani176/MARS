GPT (Generative Pre-trained Transformer) is a family of autoregressive language models developed by OpenAI. Where BERT (discussed earlier) reads text bidirectionally to understand language, GPT reads left-to-right — predicting each next token given all preceding tokens — which makes it naturally suited for *generating* text rather than just classifying or extracting from it.

The original GPT (Radford & Narasimhan, 2018) demonstrated that generative pre-training on a large unlabeled corpus, followed by discriminative fine-tuning on specific tasks, could outperform task-specific architectures. It improved state of the art on 9 of 12 benchmarks, including an 8.9% absolute improvement on commonsense reasoning and 5.7% on question answering. <citations>0</citations>

GPT-2 (Radford et al., 2019) scaled this to 1.5 billion parameters and showed something more surprising: language models could perform tasks *without any fine-tuning at all*. Trained on a dataset of millions of webpages called WebText, GPT-2 achieved state-of-the-art results on 7 of 8 language modeling benchmarks in a zero-shot setting and could generate coherent paragraphs of text. <citations>1</citations>

GPT-3 (Brown et al., 2020) pushed this further to 175 billion parameters — 10x larger than any previous non-sparse language model — and demonstrated that scaling dramatically improves few-shot performance. Without any gradient updates or fine-tuning, GPT-3 achieved strong results on translation, question answering, and cloze tasks simply by being given a few examples in its input context. It could also generate news articles that human evaluators had difficulty distinguishing from human-written ones. <citations>2</citations>

The key architectural difference from BERT is worth emphasizing. BERT uses a bidirectional encoder with masked language modeling — it sees context from both sides, making it strong at understanding tasks (classification, extraction, question answering). GPT uses a unidirectional decoder with autoregressive prediction — it only sees preceding context, which is a natural fit for generation tasks. As Gillioz et al. note in their survey, these two architectures represent the two main branches of transformer-based NLP: auto-encoder (BERT) and auto-regressive (GPT). <citations>3</citations>

In terms of applications, GPT models have been applied across a wide range of language generation tasks. Text summarization is a major use case, though comparative evaluations suggest GPT is not always the best choice — a 2024 comparison of transformer models on the CNN/Daily Mail benchmark found BART, PEGASUS, and T5 outperformed GPT on ROUGE-L scores (around 40-41 vs. 26.6 for GPT), though GPT was the fastest. <citations>4</citations> GPT models have also been applied to dialogue systems, where they enable few-shot learning by priming with a few conversational examples rather than requiring full fine-tuning. <citations>5</citations> Code generation is another active area — GPT-3 achieved 62% accuracy on semantic code clone generation and 91% on cross-language code clones. <citations>6</citations> Healthcare text generation, <citations>7</citations> legal text processing, <citations>8</citations> and radiology report standardization <citations>9</citations> are among the applied domains where GPT models have been evaluated.

The broader significance, as surveyed by Min et al. in *ACM Computing Surveys*, is that GPT and BERT together catalyzed a paradigm shift: from training task-specific models from scratch to pre-training a general language model once and then adapting it — via fine-tuning, prompting, or in-context learning — to downstream tasks. <citations>10</citations> GPT's contribution specifically was demonstrating that sheer scale in autoregressive models unlocks emergent capabilities like few-shot learning that smaller models lack. <citations>2</citations>
