Image segmentation — dividing an image into meaningful regions at the pixel level — comes in three distinct flavors, each answering a different question, plus a rich ecosystem of architectures that has evolved from early CNNs through to today's foundation models.

The three task types form a natural hierarchy. **Semantic segmentation** assigns a class label to every pixel ("this pixel is road, that pixel is sky") but doesn't distinguish between individual objects of the same class. **Instance segmentation** detects and segments each individual object separately ("these are three different cars") but ignores background "stuff" classes. **Panoptic segmentation**, formalized by Kirillov et al. (2018, CVPR) in a heavily cited paper, unifies both: it labels every pixel by class *and* distinguishes individual instances for countable "things" (cars, people) while treating uncountable "stuff" (sky, road) as semantic regions. <citations>0,1,2</citations>

The architectural approaches map roughly onto these task types:

**Fully Convolutional Networks (FCNs)** launched the deep learning era of segmentation by replacing fully connected layers with convolutional ones, enabling end-to-end pixel-wise classification on arbitrary input sizes. FCN was the first to effectively apply CNNs to semantic segmentation. <citations>3,4</citations> A comparative review reports FCN achieves mean IoU scores of roughly 0.35–0.55 on COCO — respectable as a baseline but outperformed by later methods. <citations>5</citations>

**Encoder-decoder architectures** improved on FCN by more carefully recovering spatial detail lost during downsampling. U-Net, with its symmetric encoder-decoder structure and skip connections that pass high-resolution features directly from encoder to decoder, became dominant in biomedical imaging and remains the go-to for many medical segmentation tasks. <citations>6,7</citations> SegNet uses a similar encoder-decoder design but stores pooling indices rather than full feature maps, making it more memory-efficient and suitable for real-time applications. <citations>5,8</citations> Comparative studies consistently find U-Net excels at precise boundary delineation, while SegNet trades some accuracy for computational efficiency. <citations>8</citations>

**Atrous/dilated convolution approaches**, most notably the DeepLab family, use atrous (dilated) convolutions to expand the receptive field without losing resolution, and atrous spatial pyramid pooling (ASPP) to capture multi-scale context. DeepLab variants achieve some of the highest IoU scores among CNN-based methods (0.60–0.80 on COCO). <citations>5,9</citations> PSPNet takes a related approach with pyramid pooling modules that aggregate context at multiple scales. <citations>10</citations>

**Region-based instance segmentation** takes a fundamentally different approach — detect objects first, then segment within each detection. Mask R-CNN extends Faster R-CNN by adding a mask prediction branch, producing per-instance segmentation masks alongside bounding boxes and class labels. It's the standard for instance segmentation and achieves IoU scores comparable to DeepLab (0.60–0.80). <citations>5</citations> Several studies comparing U-Net and Mask R-CNN find that Mask R-CNN outperforms U-Net specifically when objects overlap, since U-Net's pixel-wise prediction doesn't naturally handle overlapping instances. <citations>11</citations>

**Panoptic architectures** combine semantic and instance branches. A typical approach uses a shared feature extractor (e.g., ResNet-50) feeding both a semantic segmentation branch (often with pyramid pooling) and an instance segmentation branch (Mask R-CNN-style), then merges predictions using heuristics or learned fusion. <citations>12,13</citations> Li & Chen (2021) categorize these into top-down, bottom-up, single-path, and hybrid methods. <citations>14</citations>

The most recent paradigm shift is **foundation models for segmentation**. Meta's Segment Anything Model (SAM), released in 2023, is a promptable segmentation model trained on over 1 billion masks that can segment virtually any object in zero-shot fashion — you provide a point, box, or text prompt, and it produces a mask. <citations>15</citations> SAM 2, released in 2024 by Ravi et al. and already cited over 2,500 times, extends this to video with a streaming memory architecture, achieving better accuracy with 3× fewer user interactions than prior video segmentation approaches, while being 6× faster than SAM on images. <citations>16</citations> In biomedical imaging, SAM has sparked over 100 publications in its first six months, approaching state-of-the-art on many tasks though still falling short on certain anatomical structures. <citations>17</citations> Domain-specific adaptations like AD-SAM for autonomous driving combine SAM's ViT encoder with CNN backbones, achieving up to +22.9 mIoU improvement over vanilla SAM on Cityscapes. <citations>18</citations>

The field has thus progressed from task-specific architectures (FCN for semantic, Mask R-CNN for instance, panoptic networks for unified understanding) toward general-purpose foundation models that handle all segmentation flavors through prompting. This is a quick survey of a massive literature — each of these architecture families has its own deep body of work worth exploring further.
