The transformer is a neural network architecture introduced by Vaswani et al. in 2017 in the paper "Attention Is All You Need." It replaced the recurrent and convolutional networks that had previously dominated sequence processing, relying entirely on attention mechanisms. On machine translation benchmarks, the original transformer achieved 28.4 BLEU on English-to-German and 41.8 on English-to-French — state-of-the-art results — while training in just 3.5 days on eight GPUs, a fraction of what competing models required. <citations>0</citations>

The architecture has an **encoder-decoder** structure, and its core innovation is the **self-attention mechanism**. Here's how the key components work:

**Self-attention** lets each position in a sequence attend to every other position, computing relevance scores across the entire input simultaneously. Concretely, each token is projected into three vectors — a *query*, a *key*, and a *value*. The attention score between any two tokens is the dot product of the query of one with the key of the other, scaled by the square root of the key dimension. These scores are passed through a softmax to produce weights, and the output for each position is a weighted sum of all value vectors. This means the model can directly connect any two positions regardless of distance, solving the long-range dependency problem that plagued RNNs. <citations>1,2</citations>

**Multi-head attention** runs several self-attention operations in parallel (typically 8 or 16 "heads"), each with different learned projections. This allows the model to attend to information from different representation subspaces simultaneously — one head might capture syntactic relationships while another captures semantic ones. The outputs are concatenated and linearly projected. <citations>1</citations>

**Positional encoding** is necessary because, unlike RNNs, transformers process all positions in parallel and have no inherent notion of order. The original transformer uses sinusoidal functions of different frequencies to encode position, adding these to the input embeddings so the model can distinguish where tokens appear in the sequence. Later work has explored relative positional encodings, which encode the distance between tokens rather than absolute position — Rosendahl et al. found relative encoding is substantially better (4-11% BLEU improvement) when translating sentences longer than any seen during training. <citations>3</citations>

**The encoder** stacks multiple identical layers, each containing a multi-head self-attention sublayer followed by a position-wise feed-forward network (two linear transformations with a ReLU activation between them). Residual connections and layer normalization wrap each sublayer. The encoder processes the entire input in parallel, producing contextualized representations.

**The decoder** is similar but adds a third sublayer: cross-attention (or encoder-decoder attention), where the decoder attends to the encoder's output. The decoder's self-attention is also *masked* — it can only attend to earlier positions, preventing it from "seeing the future" during generation. <citations>1</citations>

The reason this architecture was so consequential goes beyond its performance on translation. Because self-attention processes all positions in parallel rather than sequentially (as RNNs must), transformers are far more parallelizable and therefore much faster to train on modern hardware. <citations>0</citations> This parallelizability is what made it feasible to scale models to billions of parameters — enabling BERT (which uses the encoder stack) and GPT (which uses the decoder stack), as we discussed earlier.

The architecture has since expanded well beyond NLP. Vision Transformers (ViT) apply the same self-attention mechanism to image patches for tasks like image recognition, object detection, and segmentation. <citations>4,5</citations> Transformers have also been applied to speech recognition, robotics, and multimodal settings. <citations>6</citations> A known limitation is the quadratic computational complexity of self-attention — it scales as $$O(N^2)$$ with sequence length — which has driven research into efficient variants for long sequences. <citations>7</citations>
